## Abstract

Insurance stress tests commonly apply predefined shocks or simulate losses from static frequency–severity models. These approaches estimate adverse outcomes but do not necessarily integrate scenario sequencing, structural consistency, deterministic replay, and failure attribution. This paper proposes ARCCRAFT (Actuarial Risk & Contingency Computational, Reproducible Architecture for Failure Testing), a versioned procedural architecture in which synthetic insurance worlds evolve over multiple periods under explicit states, transitions, bounds, stochastic streams, and failure predicates. The evaluation separates computational verification from empirical prediction. First, a retrospective motor-insurance study uses 354,140 policy-year observations from 2022–2024 and two temporal forecast origins. Policy-level Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood frequency modelling reduces held-out count deviance from 0.8481 to 0.7871 in 2023 and from 0.9153 to 0.8330 in 2024, but does not improve every aggregate error. A policy-level cost model is unstable across years: its incurred-loss error is +9.58% in 2023 and −4.44% in 2024. No model covers observed incurred loss with its process-only interval, and no model covers the 2024 claim count. Second, a synthetic ablation study using three seeds and 10,000 worlds per configuration establishes exact local replay and shows that named PCG64 substreams isolate claim and behavioural results from irrelevant world-stream consumption. Validator and transition ablations alter the selected population and coded failure rates, but the full model’s failures are almost entirely generated by its operational traceability gate. The results support ARCCRAFT as a reproducible audit and diagnostic architecture in the inspected environment; they do not establish calibrated scenario plausibility, general predictive superiority, causal failure attribution, or external validity. Independent portfolios, claim-level losses, operational labels, and prospectively untouched forecast periods are required for confirmatory validation.

**Keywords:** actuarial stress testing; procedural simulation; non-life insurance; model risk; reproducibility; reverse stress testing; predictive calibration.

## 1. Introduction

Actuarial decisions under stress require more than an estimate of expected loss. Insurers and regulators also need to know how adverse conditions interact over time, which combinations cross operational or solvency thresholds, whether a critical result can be replayed, and which assumptions or events are associated with failure. Conventional frequency–severity models and Monte Carlo simulations remain essential for loss projection, but the surrounding workflow often treats scenario construction, validation, random-number governance, and diagnostic attribution as separate activities.

This separation creates several risks. A simulated outcome can be numerically extreme but structurally inconsistent. A scenario can be labelled severe and plausible without a quantitative plausibility criterion. A result can be reproducible under one execution order yet change after an unrelated component consumes random numbers. A compact point error can coexist with poor predictive-interval coverage. Finally, a failure record can identify the threshold that was crossed without establishing the real-world cause of that failure. These properties—loss projection, scenario plausibility, computational reproducibility, predictive calibration, and attribution—must therefore be evaluated separately.

ARCCRAFT is proposed as an integration architecture for this problem. It represents a synthetic environment as a versioned, time-dependent world; allocates independent pseudorandom streams to world generation, claims, and behaviour; validates initial states through explicit pass, repair, or reject decisions; simulates multi-period outcomes; and records failed worlds in a seed-addressable Failure Atlas. The framework also includes a local reverse-stress search. These components build on established work rather than constituting a new actuarial theory.

The research question is: **Does integrating explicit multi-period transitions, structural validation, separated random streams, replay semantics, and failure attribution produce measurable improvements in reproducibility, diagnostic information, failure detection, or empirical prediction relative to transparent comparators, and at what cost?** The evaluation is deliberately falsifiable. ARCCRAFT may add governance value without improving predictive accuracy, and individual architectural components may have little effect under the current grammar.

This study makes four bounded contributions. First, it specifies the implemented architecture and maps its claims to executable functions and versioned outputs. Second, it audits a complete motor-insurance table and implements matched aggregate and policy-level actuarial comparators over two temporal origins. Third, it tests local deterministic replay, stream isolation, validator policy, state transitions, and runtime through controlled ablations. Fourth, it reports negative findings—including interval misses, decile miscalibration, lack of policy-severity superiority, and dominance of the traceability gate—rather than defining validation from favourable point estimates.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses

### 2.1 Frequency–severity and longitudinal actuarial models

Non-life loss modelling conventionally separates claim frequency from severity because counts contain many zeros while positive costs are right-skewed. The conditional-independence assumption is convenient but can be restrictive when frequency and severity share observed or latent drivers. Garrido et al. (2016), Shi et al. (2015), Frees et al. (2016), Lee and Shi (2019), Jeong and Valdez (2020), and Gao and Li (2023) show several ways to represent dependence across outcomes or time. These studies imply that a procedural model cannot be judged solely against a weak static mean: it must be compared with transparent policy-level and longitudinal alternatives under the same information set.

### 2.2 Stress design and plausibility

Supervisory stress testing requires clear objectives, material risks, and severe-but-plausible scenarios (EIOPA, 2020). Systematic scenario-selection methods formalize the trade-off between damage and plausibility through admissible regions, entropy, or empirical likelihood (Breuer et al., 2009; Breuer & Csiszar, 2013; Glasserman et al., 2015). A structural validator can enforce coded bounds, but internal consistency is not statistical plausibility. Repairing a world can also change its marginal and joint distribution. Validator acceptance, repair, and rejection are therefore model outputs that must be disclosed.

Reverse stress testing begins from a failure condition and seeks changes capable of producing it. Pesenti et al. (2019) derive minimally distorted probability measures under output stresses, while Kroell et al. (2024) extend stress analysis to dynamic loss processes. ARCCRAFT does not claim novelty for reverse or dynamic stress testing. Its narrower proposition is that seed-level world records and replayable failure predicates may improve auditability. Association and attribution are the appropriate terms unless implanted ground truth or a causal design supports stronger language.

### 2.3 Synthetic environments, reproducibility, and validation

Synthetic insurance data and artificial insurers are established research resources. The Individual Claims History Simulation Machine, SynthETIC, SPLICE, and openIRM provide controlled claim processes or risk-model environments for benchmarking (Gabrielli & Wüthrich, 2018; Avanzi et al., 2021, 2023; Wolf et al., 2026). ARCCRAFT's scientific contribution cannot therefore be the use of simulation itself; it must lie in the tested integration and measurable consequences of its components.

Reproducibility is necessary but not equivalent to validity. Long independent random streams and substreams are established simulation-design practices (L'Ecuyer et al., 2002). Reproducible computational research further requires code, data transformations, configurations, and environment information (Peng, 2011; Stodden, 2015; Stodden et al., 2016). Simulation validation additionally asks whether the conceptual and computerized models are suitable for the intended use (Sargent, 2013). Exact replay can verify an engineering contract without demonstrating that scenario probabilities or transition laws are realistic.

Forecast evaluation similarly separates calibration from sharpness and requires scores appropriate to the outcome type (Gneiting et al., 2007; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007; Czado et al., 2009). Stable tail backtesting requires repeated forecast–outcome pairs (Kupiec, 1995; Christoffersen, 1998; Nolde & Ziegel, 2017). One outcome beyond a 97.5% quantile is negative evidence for that forecast, but it cannot estimate long-run tail coverage.

### 2.4 Testable expectations

The evaluation addresses the following expectations:

- **H1:** a registered seed reproduces the same worlds, metrics, and failure labels in the inspected environment;
- **H2:** named substreams isolate claims and behaviour from irrelevant consumption of the world stream;
- **H3:** the validator changes the accepted scenario population and reported failures;
- **H4:** multi-period state transitions change failure metrics relative to fixed validated states;
- **H5:** policy-level models improve held-out predictive performance relative to pooled means;
- **H6:** the Failure Atlas exposes replayable coded failure conditions, but causal diagnostic value remains unsupported without external or implanted ground truth.

H5 is evaluated dimension by dimension rather than through a post-hoc composite score.

## 3. Research methodology

### 3.1 Study design and evidence layers

The study has two evidence layers. The empirical layer evaluates aggregate and policy-level forecasts on a public motor portfolio. The procedural layer evaluates the internal behaviour of the synthetic ARCCRAFT engine. The layers are intentionally not conflated: the motor benchmark does not empirically validate the synthetic world grammar, and synthetic replay does not validate the motor predictions.

The protocol is retrospective. The 2024 outcome and an earlier implementation were inspected before the final policy-level specification was frozen. The final transformations, predictors, penalty, temporal origins, and metrics were recorded in `16_RETROSPECTIVE_ANALYSIS_SPECIFICATION.md` before executing the reported final notebook, but the analysis is not preregistered or confirmatory.

### 3.2 Motor data and quality controls

The normalized SQLite table contains 354,140 policy-year records for 185,678 insured identifiers from 2022–2024 and 47 fields documented in the supplied dictionary. The database passed its integrity check, its schema exactly matched the dictionary, no duplicate insured-year keys or exact duplicate groups were detected, and premium, claim-count, and incurred totals reconciled with their recorded components.

Quality controls covered completeness, range, categorical levels, internal consistency, temporal composition, and leakage. Missingness affected 513 vehicle values, two vehicle ages, and two licence values. One zero-exposure record had reported claims and was excluded from frequency fitting. Among 50,807 claim-bearing records, 8,543 (16.81%) had zero incurred cost; consequently, a positive-only Gamma severity model was not appropriate. The field `age_driving_licence` is described as a calendar year but ranges from 0 to 80, so it was retained only as an unidentified numeric proxy. `policy_status` was excluded because current cancellation status may contain post-inception information. The undocumented `NA` fuel category was retained as an explicit level. Portfolio rows increased from 67,172 in 2022 to 168,133 in 2024, and the largest business-type share moved by 40.57 percentage points, indicating material temporal composition change.

### 3.3 Empirical comparators

Two rolling temporal origins were used: 2022 to predict 2023, and 2022–2023 to predict 2024. Target-year policy characteristics and exposure were treated as known at forecast inception. Three specifications shared the same data partitions:

1. **B0, aggregate pooled:** pooled claims per unit exposure and pooled incurred loss per reported claim;
2. **B1, policy frequency with pooled severity:** a policy-level frequency model and pooled cost per claim;
3. **B2, policy frequency with policy severity:** policy-level models for both frequency and incurred cost per reported claim.

For policy (i), frequency was fitted by penalized Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood,

\[
\mu_i^{N}=E_i\exp(x_i^{\top}\beta),
\]

where (E_i>0) is exposure. The cost model used total incurred amount as the response and reported claim count as exposure,

\[
\mu_i^{L}=N_i\exp(x_i^{\top}\gamma),
\]

on claim-bearing calibration rows. This is a non-negative conditional-mean model and not a literal claim-level Poisson distribution for currency. It accommodates zero-incurred claim rows but cannot recover individual loss severity from policy-year aggregates.

The design matrix included driver age, vehicle age, the ambiguous licence proxy, log vehicle value, seats, log power-to-weight ratio, selected squared terms, and categorical indicators for policy type, business type, payment frequency, bonus score, fuel, vehicle brand, municipality type, and circulation area. Numerical values were median-imputed and clipped at calibration-period 0.5% and 99.5% quantiles before standardization. Rare brands were pooled. The L2 penalty was fixed at (10^{-4}), excluding the intercept, and models were estimated by L-BFGS-B. Calendar trend and policy status were excluded.

Evaluation used aggregate relative error, policy-level Poisson deviance, risk-decile observed-to-predicted ratios, and approximate aggregate process intervals. These intervals combine Poisson count variation with the existing lognormal cost-dispersion proxy. They exclude parameter, feature, model, and distribution-shift uncertainty and are therefore labelled process-only.

### 3.4 ARCCRAFT architecture

An ARCCRAFT world contains a scenario class, regime, initial values for eleven state variables, and a twelve-period path. The current synthetic implementation allocates PCG64 substreams to `world`, `claims`, and `behaviour` using `SeedSequence.spawn`. World states are drawn around a central state and perturbed by regime and scenario-class intensity. Scenario classes are baseline, alternative, adverse, severe-plausible, and exploratory; reverse stress is produced by a separate local search. Because no external probability or divergence criterion calibrates the labels, “severe-plausible” denotes a configured class name rather than demonstrated economic plausibility.

The validator first applies hard bounds that trigger rejection and then soft bounds that clip accepted states and record repair. Simulation uses copies of validated state arrays. Premium, claims, and a payment-traceability score accumulate over twelve periods while behavioural noise updates state variables when transitions are active. A world fails first on a non-compensatory traceability threshold of 0.95 and otherwise on a combined-ratio threshold of 1.0. The Failure Atlas stores the world identifier, regime, class, failed gate, output values, master seed, and stable substream descriptors. It currently records terminal gate attribution, not the full first-failure trajectory or a causal mechanism.

### 3.5 Procedural ablations

Five configurations were executed for master seeds 20260825, 20260826, and 20260827 with 10,000 worlds per seed:

- full ARCCRAFT;
- no validator, passing raw initial states into the simulator;
- strict rejection, changing every repair into rejection;
- no transitions, holding validated initial states fixed over all twelve periods;
- shared stream, replacing the three named substreams with one sequential PCG64 generator.

Every configuration was rerun and compared by a SHA-256 fingerprint of labels and numerical outputs. Stream isolation was tested by consuming 100 unused random draws after world generation and before simulation. The intervention should not affect claims or behaviour under separated streams but should affect a sequential shared stream. Outcomes included accepted sample size, repair and rejection counts, failure rate among accepted worlds, failure-or-rejection rate, gate-specific failures, mean combined ratio, mean traceability, rank correlation screening, and wall-clock runtime. Runtime is host-specific and descriptive.

## 4. Results and findings

### 4.1 Data integrity and temporal shift

The database was mechanically consistent at the insured-year grain, but semantic and temporal risks were material. The mismatch between the licence-field definition and observed values prevented a substantive interpretation of that predictor. Zero-incurred claim rows ruled out an unmodified positive-only severity model. The rapid portfolio growth and composition shift made random row splitting inappropriate and justified temporal evaluation. These limitations were handled in modelling but cannot be removed without the source documentation and claim-level records.

Policy-level frequency improved held-out count deviance in both years and improved the 2023 aggregate count error slightly. Its 2024 aggregate count error was slightly worse than B0. B1 also achieved lower incurred deviance because differentiated count predictions change expected incurred cost even with pooled severity.

B2 did not demonstrate severity-model superiority. It reduced the absolute 2024 incurred error by 0.43 percentage points relative to B0, but increased the 2023 error from +2.70% to +9.58% and had higher incurred deviance than B1 in both years. This temporal instability is a negative result.

Calibration-by-decile also qualified the deviance improvement. In the lowest predicted count-risk decile, observed-to-predicted ratios were 1.31 in 2023 and 1.44 in 2024. Incurred-cost ratios in the lowest decile were approximately 1.68 and 1.93, respectively. The miss was therefore structured across risk groups rather than confined to aggregate sampling noise. All incurred totals fell outside their process-only intervals, and all three 2024 count forecasts missed their intervals. With only two origins, long-run interval coverage and tail calibration remain unidentifiable.

### 4.3 Synthetic engine and Failure Atlas

The separate aggregate sensitivity analysis produced a monotone 5 by 5 frequency and severity response surface under the implemented multiplicative shocks. At a joint frequency multiplier of 1.25 and severity multiplier of 1.25, the mean loss ratio was 1.10985, with a 95% process interval from 1.09131 to 1.12822. This verifies the configured response and identifies a technical failure region, but it does not establish that the joint shock is historically calibrated or economically plausible.

Panel B follows the equal multiplier path and shows the 95% process interval; the joint 1.25 multiplier produces a mean loss ratio of 1.11, above the technical threshold of 1.00. Source: registered ARCCRAFT stress simulations using the retrospective motor calibration.

The canonical full run at seed 20260825 yielded 8,037 pass, 1,958 repair, and five reject worlds. The Failure Atlas retained 3,588 traceability failures, one combined-ratio failure, and all five structural rejects. The reverse-stress search found a configured failure at standardized distance 1.0123, with traceability 0.9321 and combined ratio 0.4017. This is a local search result under the synthetic grammar, not proof of a globally minimal or economically plausible stress.

The imbalance between 3,588 traceability failures and one combined-ratio failure is central to interpretation. The present experiment mainly evaluates an operational threshold embedded in the simulator; it offers almost no statistical power for comparing actuarial failure detection. Mean combined ratios around 0.44 in the ablation runs are also far below the failure threshold.

### 4.4 Reproducibility, ablation, and cost

All 15 configuration–seed pairs replayed with identical fingerprints. Under the full configuration, consuming 100 unused world-stream draws before simulation left the fingerprint unchanged. Under the shared-stream configuration, the same intervention changed the fingerprint. H1 and H2 are therefore supported as local computational properties.

| Configuration | Mean accepted-world failure rate | SD across three seeds | Mean failure-or-reject rate | Mean combined ratio | Mean runtime for 10,000 worlds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Full | 35.95% | 0.24 pp | 35.98% | 0.4436 | 3.59 s |
| No validator | 35.79% | 0.22 pp | 35.79% | 0.4436 | 4.74 s |
| Strict reject | 36.83% | 0.34 pp | 49.41% | 0.4363 | 3.21 s |
| No transitions | 35.70% | 0.25 pp | 35.73% | 0.4432 | 3.50 s |
| Shared stream | 35.20% | 0.69 pp | 35.24% | 0.4429 | 3.97 s |

The full validator repaired an average of 1,986.3 worlds and rejected 4.7 per seed. Strict rejection accepted only the pass population and increased the total failure-or-rejection rate to 49.41%. This supports H3 only in the narrow sense that validator policy changes selection and reporting; no external criterion establishes that repair creates more plausible worlds.

Disabling transitions lowered the accepted-world failure rate by 0.25 percentage points relative to the full model. This supports H4 as a detectable computational effect, but the effect is small under the current grammar and three-seed design. The shared-stream failure-rate difference is not interpretable as an actuarial effect because sharing changes the random-number coupling. Its relevant result is the failed isolation test, not the lower observed rate.

Runtime differences were modest and noisy. The full pipeline averaged 3.59 seconds, while configuration means ranged from 3.21 to 4.74 seconds. Removing the validator was not faster in these wall-clock observations, indicating that incidental runtime variation and changed downstream calculation dominate the validator's small direct cost. These timings should not be generalized across hosts.

## 5. Discussion

The study supports a narrower contribution than the phrase “validated framework” would imply. ARCCRAFT provides a locally reproducible computational architecture that makes seeds, validation decisions, scenario classes, and failure gates explicit. The stream-isolation intervention supplies stronger evidence than a same-seed rerun alone: it demonstrates that unrelated world-stream consumption does not silently change claims or behaviour. This property is useful for controlled model development and audit.

The empirical benchmark gives mixed evidence. Policy-level frequency improves discrimination as measured by held-out deviance, consistent with established actuarial regression practice, but does not uniformly improve aggregate errors or calibration. The policy-level cost model fails the intended superiority test because its 2023 deterioration is large and its decile calibration is poor. The result illustrates why aggregate error, individual deviance, group calibration, and interval coverage must remain separate.

The procedural ablations also delimit the architectural claims. Repair versus rejection materially changes the effective sample and the reported failure-or-rejection rate. The validator is therefore part of the data-generating process, not a neutral cleaning step. Yet the experiment provides no statistical or economic plausibility criterion for choosing repair over rejection. Similarly, state transitions affect results, but only modestly under the present parameterization. A stronger claim would require empirically calibrated dynamics, matched simulation budgets, and uncertainty estimates across more grammars and seeds.

The Failure Atlas succeeds as an audit record of coded failures. It does not yet identify real-world causes. Its current records do not contain an empirically observed failure label, implanted causal mechanism, or full first-failure time. Controlled fault-injection experiments should therefore be added before using causal language or claiming improved failure detection.

For management and model-risk governance, the architecture's immediate value is procedural: it makes assumptions and failure definitions inspectable, exposes the distribution-changing effect of validation, and supports deterministic replay. These properties can strengthen model challenge even when predictive performance is unchanged. Operational value, however, requires user studies, deployment evidence, or governance-case evaluation that is not available here.

### Limitations

The motor data cover only three annual periods, the final year was already inspected, and the original raw motor file is absent even though its recorded hash is available. Policy-year incurred totals do not identify individual claim severity. Process intervals omit parameter and model uncertainty. The full synthetic grammar is not calibrated to the motor portfolio, and scenario class probabilities, transitions, dependencies, and thresholds are assumptions. Only three ablation seeds were timed on one host. Combined-ratio failures are too rare for useful comparison. No external operational labels or independent portfolio are available. These constraints prevent claims of general predictive validation, stable tail coverage, causal attribution, or external validity.

## 6. Conclusion and recommendations

ARCCRAFT is best characterized by the present evidence as a proposed reproducible audit architecture for procedural actuarial stress testing. In the inspected local environment, its registered simulations replay exactly, its named streams provide demonstrable component isolation, and its validator and transition rules have observable consequences. These findings support computational verification and transparent model governance.

The evidence does not support overall predictive superiority. Policy-level frequency modelling improves held-out count deviance but remains temporally miscalibrated, while policy-level severity is unstable. The 2024 outcome falls outside all reported process-only intervals, and the synthetic experiment is dominated by its traceability threshold. The paper therefore rejects complete validation and treats the empirical results as retrospective preliminary evidence.

Four next steps are required. First, evaluate a prospectively untouched year or independent portfolio. Second, obtain claim-level payments, expenses, commissions, and operational traceability outcomes so that loss tails and failure gates can be validated empirically. Third, calibrate scenario dynamics and plausibility criteria rather than relying on labels. Fourth, conduct registered fault-injection experiments and compare diagnostic precision, recall, delay, and attribution rank against conventional simulation and sensitivity methods. Until those steps are completed, ARCCRAFT's strongest demonstrated contribution is reproducible procedural integration, not established actuarial superiority.
