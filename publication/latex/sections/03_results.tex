\section{Results and findings}\label{sec:results-and-findings}

\subsection{Data integrity and temporal shift}\label{sec:data-integrity-and-temporal-shift}

The database was mechanically consistent at the insured-year grain, but semantic and temporal risks were material. The mismatch between the licence-field definition and observed values prevented a substantive interpretation of that predictor. Zero-incurred claim rows ruled out an unmodified positive-only severity model. The rapid portfolio growth and composition shift made random row splitting inappropriate and justified temporal evaluation. These limitations were handled in modelling but cannot be removed without the source documentation and claim-level records.

Policy-level frequency improved held-out count deviance in both years and improved the 2023 aggregate count error slightly. Its 2024 aggregate count error was slightly worse than B0. B1 also achieved lower incurred deviance because differentiated count predictions change expected incurred cost even with pooled severity.

B2 did not demonstrate severity-model superiority. It reduced the absolute 2024 incurred error by 0.43 percentage points relative to B0, but increased the 2023 error from +2.70\% to +9.58\% and had higher incurred deviance than B1 in both years. This temporal instability is a negative result.

Calibration-by-decile also qualified the deviance improvement. In the lowest predicted count-risk decile, observed-to-predicted ratios were 1.31 in 2023 and 1.44 in 2024. Incurred-cost ratios in the lowest decile were approximately 1.51 and 1.93, respectively. The miss was therefore structured across risk groups rather than confined to aggregate sampling noise. All incurred totals fell outside their process-only intervals, and all three 2024 count forecasts missed their intervals. With only two origins, long-run interval coverage and tail calibration remain unidentifiable.

\subsection{Synthetic engine and Failure Atlas}\label{sec:synthetic-engine-and-failure-atlas}

The separate aggregate sensitivity analysis produced a monotone 5 by 5 frequency and severity response surface under the implemented multiplicative shocks. At a joint frequency multiplier of 1.25 and severity multiplier of 1.25, the mean loss ratio was 1.10985, with a 95\% process interval from 1.09131 to 1.12822. This verifies the configured response and identifies a technical failure region, but it does not establish that the joint shock is historically calibrated or economically plausible.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figure5.png}
\caption{\textbf{Frequency and severity stress surface for the motor portfolio.} Panel A reports the mean simulated loss ratio for the 25 combinations of frequency and severity multipliers, with the outlined cell identifying the joint 25\% stress. Panel B follows the equal multiplier path and shows the 95\% process interval; the joint 1.25 multiplier produces a mean loss ratio of 1.11, above the technical threshold of 1.00. Source: registered ARCCRAFT stress simulations using the retrospective motor calibration.}
\label{fig:5}
\end{figure*}

The canonical full run at seed 20260825 yielded 8,037 pass, 1,958 repair, and five reject worlds. The Failure Atlas retained 3,588 traceability failures, one combined-ratio failure, and all five structural rejects. The reverse-stress search found a configured failure at standardized distance 1.0123, with traceability 0.9321 and combined ratio 0.4017. This is a local search result under the synthetic grammar, not proof of a globally minimal or economically plausible stress.

The imbalance between 3,588 traceability failures and one combined-ratio failure is central to interpretation. The present experiment mainly evaluates an operational threshold embedded in the simulator; it offers almost no statistical power for comparing actuarial failure detection. Mean combined ratios around 0.44 in the ablation runs are also far below the failure threshold.

\subsection{Reproducibility, ablation, and cost}\label{sec:reproducibility-ablation-and-cost}

All 15 configuration-seed pairs replayed with identical fingerprints. Under the full configuration, consuming 100 unused world-stream draws before simulation left the fingerprint unchanged. Under the shared-stream configuration, the same intervention changed the fingerprint. H1 and H2 are therefore supported as local computational properties.

The full validator repaired an average of 1,986.3 worlds and rejected 4.7 per seed. Strict rejection accepted only the pass population and increased the total failure-or-rejection rate to 49.41\%. This supports H3 only in the narrow sense that validator policy changes selection and reporting; no external criterion establishes that repair creates more plausible worlds.

Disabling transitions lowered the accepted-world failure rate by 0.25 percentage points relative to the full model. This supports H4 as a detectable computational effect, but the effect is small under the current grammar and three-seed design. The shared-stream failure-rate difference is not interpretable as an actuarial effect because sharing changes the random-number coupling. Its relevant result is the failed isolation test, not the lower observed rate.

Runtime differences were modest and noisy. The full pipeline averaged 3.59 seconds, while configuration means ranged from 3.21 to 4.74 seconds. Removing the validator was not faster in these wall-clock observations, indicating that incidental runtime variation and changed downstream calculation dominate the validator's small direct cost. These timings should not be generalized across hosts.
